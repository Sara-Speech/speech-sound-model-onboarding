Your model problem

Given audio of a known target speech sound, decide whether that sound was produced correctly.

Input: sound audio Known: target sound Primary output: correct / incorrect Secondary: substitution / distortion / rare addition Scope: 23 supported sounds

From recording to prediction

1 · TargetA known speech sound, such as /s/, /r/, or voiced “th.”
2 · AudioA recording of that sound is provided to the model.
3 · EvaluateThe model compares the production with what is expected for the target sound.
4 · DecisionCorrect or incorrect; optionally, the specific error type.

Current task: sound level

The model receives audio of a target sound and determines whether that sound was produced correctly.

Previous work: word level

IPA, in 60 seconds

IPA is a sound alphabet

Ordinary letters describe spelling. The International Phonetic Alphabet gives each speech sound a consistent symbol, so the team can discuss what was actually produced.

What the slashes mean

A symbol between slashes—such as /s/—names the target sound category. You need to recognize the target symbols; you are not expected to transcribe recordings from scratch.

/ʃ/“sh,” as in shoe
/tʃ/“ch,” as in chair
/ð/voiced “th,” as in this
/θ/voiceless “th,” as in think
/ŋ/“ng,” as in sing
/j/“y,” as in yes

IPA is about sound, not spelling: the IPA symbol /j/ represents the English “y” sound, not the letter J.

Label vocabulary

CorrectThe target is acceptably produced in its intended location.Sound example: target /s/ → heard /s/
SubstitutionAnother recognizable sound replaces the target.Sound example: target /s/ → heard /θ/ (“th”)
OmissionWord-level onlyThe target sound is absent from a word. This label does not apply to the active sound-level detection task.Word example: “base” /beɪs/ → /beɪ/
DistortionThe target is present but atypical; it does not cleanly become another phoneme.Sound example: target /s/ → distorted /s/ that is still heard as “s”
AdditionRare at sound levelAn extra sound is produced alongside the target. This can occur in sound-level detection, but it is very rare.Rare sound example: target /s/ → heard /sə/ with an added vowel

For the current sound-level task: the main labels are Correct, Substitution, and Distortion. Addition is possible but very rare. Omission appears only to help interpret the previous word-level materials.

Where labels and models go wrong

Signal quality

Noise, echo, clipping, inconsistent duration, or voice artifacts can be mistaken for incorrect sound production.

Sound identity

The model may confuse acoustically similar sounds, including voiced and voiceless pairs or sounds with nearby places of articulation.

Label boundaries

An ambiguous production may be forced into a substitution label when the audio supports only distortion or uncertainty.

Evaluation

One overall score can hide weak sounds. False positives—marking an incorrect production correct—are especially damaging to SLP trust.

Checklist for every example

  • What is the target sound?
  • What should a correct production sound like?
  • What do you actually hear?
  • Was the target sound produced correctly?
  • Which error label best fits?
  • Is the recording technically usable?
  • Could a synthetic artifact be driving the decision?
  • Has an SLP approved the perceived label?

What the model is—and isn’t

The model supports an SLP; it does not diagnose or replace clinical judgment.

Synthetic adult speech is model-development material, not proof that an error represents a child’s speech. Dialect and multilingual differences must not automatically be labeled as disorders.

The SLP-reviewed perceived production is the gold label—not the filename or intended generation prompt.

Learn more about speech sound problems

Optional reading for anyone who wants more domain context. Start with the first ASHA guide; use the others when a term or error pattern needs clarification.

Articulation: producing a sound

This is the physical production of an individual speech sound—how the lips, tongue, airflow, and voice work together. A distorted /s/ is a sound-level articulation example.

Phonology: organizing a sound system

This concerns rule-like patterns across sounds, word positions, or syllable shapes—for example, consistently replacing back sounds with front sounds. These patterns are usually evaluated across words and connected speech.

Connection to this project: the model observes a narrow slice—audio of one known target sound. It can estimate whether that production is correct, but it cannot by itself diagnose a speech sound disorder or establish a word-level phonological pattern.
